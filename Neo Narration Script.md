# Neo’s guided Mars expedition
## Narration script for Claude Code

Neo is a fictional robot who revisits NASA’s silent equipment on Mars and opens the records it left behind. Speak the quoted lines only. Scene directions and screen labels are separate. Read alongside PRD v0.2. This script incorporates the latest continuous-tour direction.

### Playback

- One press of **Start with Neo** begins the tour. Within each chapter, camera moves, rover travel, and science reveals advance automatically. End each chapter on its final record with **Next rover** and **Explore the globe**.
- Aim for two to three minutes per chapter at a comfortable spoken pace. Time the actual read and adjust scene duration to fit. Use roomy local replay scenes with visible travel and foreground landmarks. Label them **Schematic · distances and timing simplified**. Show NASA distances in separate overlays.
- For each clue, settle the camera, briefly highlight the object, then start the relevant line. Hold the shot until the line and reading time have elapsed. The highlighted object and a clearly labeled button offer optional inspection. Inspection pauses the tour and returns to the same cue on close.
- Use the cue IDs below to synchronize camera, narration, caption, and visual state. Support pause, resume, skip, and replay. Stop stale speech on seek or mission switch. Text is always available. If audio is unavailable, use equivalent timed captions. In reduced motion, use still compositions and immediate cuts.
- Neo speaks as a present-day observer. Label historical scenes **Mission replay**. Any source-gated cue marked **Approval** needs approval before release. Use the specified fallback when it is pending.

## Opening

**intro · Globe, then fly to Ares Vallis**

> I’m Neo. I visit the machines NASA left on Mars. Their radios have gone quiet, but Earth still has what they sent back. Come look with me.

## Pathfinder & Sojourner

**pf-arrival · Establish Ares Vallis. Begin the landing replay.**

> Our first stop is Ares Vallis, a rocky plain shaped by ancient floods. Pathfinder landed here on July 4, 1997. Watch what happens when it touches down.

**pf-bounce · Airbag impact, bounce arcs, and roll to rest. Show measurements as they become relevant.**

> Those airbags cushioned Pathfinder’s landing. It bounced about fifteen times, some as high as fifty feet. It came to rest about a kilometer from where it first hit.

**pf-ramp · Lower the ramp automatically. Show 20° beside the hinge and 1 ft / 23 lb on the rover card.**

> Here comes Sojourner. It stood about one foot tall. Watch its wheels as it comes down the ramp.

**pf-pebble-look · Drive to the illustrated pebbles. Lower the camera, outline one edge, and offer Inspect pebble.**

> See that pebble? Look at its edges.

**pf-pebble-reveal · Pause briefly for observation, then reveal the explanation automatically.**

> See how smooth those edges are? Those rounded pebbles helped scientists infer that water once flowed here.

**pf-dust · Move to the dust area and zoom into an illustrated sample of airborne particles. Show the magnetic response.**

> Some of the dust floating in the Martian air was magnetic. Watch what happens in this illustration.

**pf-cloud · Tilt from the surface to the dawn sky and reveal a low cloud layer.**

> The mission also spotted clouds made of water ice early in the morning.

**pf-final · Return to the lander and show the signal traveling toward Earth. Hold on September 27, 1997 and 2.3 billion bits returned.**

> Earth received Pathfinder’s last data on September 27, 1997. By then, the mission had sent Earth 2.3 billion bits of information. The final transmission gives us Sojourner’s last known position. What happened to the rover after contact ended is uncertain.

**pf-record · Optional ending record. Approval for NASA panorama and Chimp annotation.**

> This is where NASA last knew Sojourner was, near a rock nicknamed Chimp.

If pending, hold on the lander and the final-transmission record. Skip the Chimp line and any precise rover placement. Do not invent a cause for the end of contact.

Sources: [NASA Mars Pathfinder](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/mars-pathfinder/) · [NASA landing-site panorama](https://science.nasa.gov/photojournal/mars-pathfinder-landing-site-and-surroundings/)

## Spirit

**sp-gusev · Establish Gusev. Trace the basin and label the possible lake as a hypothesis.**

> Why send a robot geologist here? Scientists wondered whether this huge crater once held a lake. Spirit came to look for clues.

**sp-drive · Drive from the landing site through Bonneville toward Columbia Hills. Reveal the 7.73 km / 4.8 mi lifetime odometer separately. Keep the early route plan in its own labeled overlay.**

> Spirit visited Bonneville Crater, then headed toward Columbia Hills. The drive you’re watching is one part of the journey. In all, Spirit traveled about 7.7 kilometers.

**sp-rock · Zoom in on an illustrated Columbia Hills specimen and offer Inspect rock.**

> Some rocks in Columbia Hills showed signs that water had changed them. That was one clue Spirit found by studying the rocks up close.

**sp-final · Cut to Later in the mission. Show a wheel sinking into soft soil and the winter light fading. Show the two dates separately.**

> Soft soil stopped Spirit’s drive. As winter approached, its solar panels had less energy to work with. Earth last heard from Spirit on March 22, 2010. NASA kept trying to reach it and closed the mission in May 2011.

**sp-troy · Approval for named site and wheel-trench inspection. If approved, replace the first sentence of sp-final with “Soft soil stopped Spirit’s drive here at Troy.” Then open the wheel-trench record and speak:**

> Even here, Spirit had something to study. Its wheels uncovered layers of soil that scientists could examine.

If pending, keep the soft-soil and winter ending with the last-contact and closure date cards. Use **May 25, 2011** on the closure card. Avoid portraying an exact moment or confirmed mechanism of hardware failure.

Sources: [NASA Spirit mission](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/mer-spirit/) · [NASA/JPL soil at Troy](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/nasa-trapped-mars-rover-finds-evidence-of-subsurface-water/) · [NASA/JPL mission closure](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/nasas-spirit-rover-completes-mission-on-mars/)

## Opportunity

**op-meridiani · Highlight the plains from above, then descend to Eagle Crater.**

> Before Opportunity landed, observations from orbit had already pointed scientists toward mineral deposits here. They wanted to see what those minerals could tell them about water.

**op-outcrop · Magnify the landing outcrop and offer Inspect outcrop. Approval for the specific hematite-rich spherule visual and line.**

> See those tiny spheres? Opportunity studied what they were made of. They helped scientists piece together evidence that this place was once wet.

If pending, retain the Meridiani site-selection explanation and the shared science conclusion. Skip the spheres.

**op-eagle · Move from the landing outcrop up to the rim and onward toward Endurance. Offer Look back as an optional inspection. Show about 750 m as the separation between craters.**

> That’s Eagle Crater behind us. See where we started? Opportunity climbed out and headed toward Endurance Crater.

**op-distance · Animate the 45.16 km and 7.73 km odometer bars from the same baseline.**

> You’ve seen one early drive. Over its lifetime, Opportunity traveled just over forty-five kilometers. For a time, that was farther than any rover had driven off Earth.

**op-final · Cut to Later in the mission. Hold on the rover at Perseverance Valley as an illustrated dust storm dims the sun.**

> A planet-wide dust storm darkened the sky here in Perseverance Valley. Less sunlight reached Opportunity’s solar panels. On June 10, 2018, Earth received its final transmission.

**op-image · Approval for final NASA image. Reveal the incomplete frame and hold it for inspection.**

> This incomplete picture was the last data it sent.

If pending, hold the illustrated valley and show the date. Skip the image-specific line. Keep the early drive and final valley scene separated by a time cut.

Sources: [NASA Opportunity mission](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/mer-opportunity/) · [NASA MER science highlights](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/mars-exploration-rovers-spirit-and-opportunity/science-highlights/) · [NASA Opportunity’s last image](https://science.nasa.gov/resource/opportunitys-last-message/)

## Closing

**outro · Return to the globe and briefly highlight all three landing sites.**

> Sojourner’s pebbles gave us a clue about flowing water. Spirit and Opportunity found more evidence that water stayed on the surface long ago. Their radios are quiet now. We can still look at what they found.

Source: [NASA Mars Exploration Rovers overview](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/mars-exploration-rovers-spirit-and-opportunity/)

## Neo in Three.js

Use `Neospritesheet1.jpeg` for gestures and expressions, and `Neospritesheet2.jpeg` for front, side, and back proportions and the optional error face. The JPEGs are design references. Build Neo as a fully modeled, rotatable Three.js character. Locate the references in the repository or the files supplied alongside this script. If Claude cannot access a reference, continue using the description below and report the missing file. Preserve the original sheets.

### Model and staging

- Create a reusable `NeoCharacter` group with a rounded blue body, pale lower shell and armor trim, dark rounded faceplate, cyan emissive eyes and mouth, a cyan chest light, antenna, and two oval arms. Use simple meshes. Give the head and each arm independent pivots. Shape the back and side from the turnaround. Render eye and mouth shapes with geometry or a local CanvasTexture.
- Render Neo using the existing Three.js renderer. Stage him near the edge of the shot with enough room for the rover and the clue. Use world-space gaze targets for the rover, clue, horizon, and viewer. Keep speech, captions, and controls in HTML. In tight layouts, frame Neo in a reserved corner of the same canvas using a scissored view if needed. Keep Neo out of globe picking.
- Add a subtle hover, head turns, one-shot arm gestures, and simple speech mouth shapes driven by the active speaking state. Use the narration cue and playback clock as the source of truth. Start a gesture when its cue begins or at a defined cue offset. On pause, inspection, seek, or mission switch, freeze or reconstruct the current pose from playback state and cancel superseded transitions. No audio or lip-sync dependency.

| `outro` | Return gaze to the viewer and close with a warm smile and small wave. |

### Fallbacks and checks

- Use a simplified mesh with readable eyes and arms if detailed geometry or face rendering fails. If WebGL fails, keep the tour and captions available in HTML and use a cropped front-view still if one is available. In reduced motion, snap to cue poses and suppress hover and gestures.
- Reserve the red glitch expression from sheet 2 for an actual guide error. Mission endings use quiet expressions so the visual stays focused on what happened to the rover.
- Verify the full tour on desktop and mobile. Check that Neo directs attention to the correct object without obscuring it, that optional inspections resume at the same beat, and that paused, skipped, or muted narration leaves the character and captions synchronized.
